<details>
<summary>Map overview and research process</summary>

<p>This project maps the physical, environmental, and labor networks that power artificial intelligence, counteracting the common myth that AI exists in an abstract, immaterial “cloud.”</p>

<p>To map these hidden geographies, I relied on Kate Crawford’s <em>Atlas of AI</em>, research publications from the AI Now Institute, environmental impact reports from Greenpeace, and investigative journalism from outlets such as TIME Magazine, Rest of World, and The Guardian.</p>

<p>The most difficult layer to uncover was the precise geographic location of outsourced data-labeling and content-moderation hubs. While major tech companies publicly identify their headquarters and data-center investments, offices housing third-party contract workers, such as Sama in Nairobi or TaskUs in Manila, are obscured by non-disclosure agreements and layers of corporate subcontracting. This difficulty reveals how companies can hide the precarious human labor required to make AI systems appear “automated” and “safe.”</p>

<p>The most striking finding was the localized ecological strain caused by data-center cooling. Discovering that servers running large language models in Des Moines, Iowa, consume millions of gallons of local drinking water during severe droughts, competing directly with surrounding agricultural communities, demonstrates the tangible footprint of LLM training. The contrast between Silicon Valley’s concentration of capital and sacrifice zones in the Global South shows AI as an extractive planetary assembly line.</p>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Artist statement</summary>

<p>This map argues that the AI “cloud” is an extractive, deeply unequal physical infrastructure rooted in environmental exploitation and outsourced neocolonial labor. By juxtaposing Silicon Valley executive hubs against mineral-extraction sites and low-wage data-moderation hubs in the Global South, the map challenges the narrative of AI as clean, immaterial, and objective technology.</p>

<p>Connecting directly to <em>Data Feminism</em> by Catherine D’Ignazio and Lauren Klein, this project uses counter-mapping to challenge institutional power asymmetries and expose the invisible labor that powers digital systems. Rather than treating map data as neutral representation, the visual design highlights who benefits financially and decisionally from AI and who bears the ecological and psychological burdens of its production.</p>

<p>If drawn by a tech CEO, this map would emphasize fiber-optic network speeds, server efficiencies, and market valuations while erasing mining conditions and the experiences of content moderators. Conversely, if drawn by a content moderator in Nairobi or a farmer in Iowa, its center of gravity would shift away from California toward zones of human and ecological sacrifice. The deliberate omission of corporate performance metrics prioritizes what the tech industry tries to render invisible.</p>
