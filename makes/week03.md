<p>The first image is a vibrant, anime-style digital illustration of me smiling in front of a campus setting. I am wearing a grey T-shirt that says “Denison Swimming &amp; Diving.” The background includes a statue of Mary with baby Jesus, a church tower, green trees, and a bright blue sky. The aesthetic is heroic, clean, and idealized, turning real-world details into an animated archetype.</p>

<p>In the second image, the AI recreated my selfie in a retro, 1980s film-photography style. I have a mullet hairstyle, wear an acid-wash denim jacket over a graphic T-shirt, and stand near a classic car and surfboard by the beach. The image features vintage film grain, warm lighting, and an ’85 timestamp in the corner, shifting my visual identity through historical nostalgia.</p>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Reflection</summary>

<p>At first, both AI images changed how I look by using different styles. The first turns me into a brightly lit, heroic anime character on a campus. The second places me in the 1980s, using old-school film grain, vintage clothes, and nostalgic details to put me in a decade I never lived in.</p>

<p>Even though both images keep some of my basic features, like my smile, they show that AI builds identity out of common patterns instead of real-life experiences. The anime picture replaces my actual personality with familiar cartoon ideas of perfection and charm. Meanwhile, the 1980s photo builds a fake memory using cultural symbols like acid-wash denim and beach gear.</p>

<p>Looking at both pictures helped me see that AI does not show the “real” me. Instead, algorithms sort, style, and rewrite who we are using popular visuals. The AI gives us cool-looking pictures, but we have to remember that these tools twist and idealize how we actually see ourselves.</p>

<p>— Natan Silva</p>
</details>
